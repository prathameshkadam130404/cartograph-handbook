Team handbook, working name Cartograph. Updated 26 September 2026.

We draw a certified map of a model nobody is allowed to open.

Banks and lenders now use AI to decide who gets a loan. Regulators say they must explain and check those decisions, even when the AI was built by someone else. We ask the AI thousands of careful questions, draw a precise map of how it behaves, and say exactly how sure we are about every part of that map.

Chapter 1

The problem, told through one loan

Most of the team will never see a model's code. You don't need to. Follow one application.

Priya applies for a ₹4 lakh personal loan on her phone

A digital lender (an NBFC) runs her details through its machine-learning model. The loan is funded jointly with a bank under a co-lending deal. The model says no.

Priya asks why. The bank's risk team asks a harder question: how do we know this model is fair, stable and behaving as the policy says, when we didn't build it and can't see inside it?

  1. The model is a sealed box.It belongs to the lending partner or a software vendor. The bank can send it applications and read the scores. That's all.
  2. The rules say the bank is still responsible.India's central bank (RBI) published draft rules in June 2026 saying banks must independently check models even when a vendor built them.
  3. Today's explanation tools are not reliable enough.A study of seven commercial tools found only some of them correctly identified what drove the model's decisions. Results changed with how the tool sampled.
  4. Manual checks are slow and expensive.US banks report paying around $75,000 or more to validate a single vendor model framework.
  5. That gap is our business.A fast, repeatable check that works from the outside, and states how accurate it is.
Chapter 2

How the product works, in seven moves

Scroll slowly. The picture on the side changes with each step. The landscape is a stand-in for the model: left to right is the applicant's income, bottom to top is their debt, and the colour is how likely the model is to approve.

A model we can only talk to from outside.
1

Start with a sealed box

We connect to the customer's model the way their own app does: we send an application, we get a score back. We never see the code or the training data.

Why this matters: banks can give us this access without legal fights over the vendor's secrets. It is also exactly the situation the new rules describe.
2

Ask smart questions

We send made-up but realistic applications. Our algorithm chooses each next question based on the answers so far, spending more questions where the model's behaviour changes quickly.

Plain version: like a surveyor who takes more measurements on a hillside than on a flat field.
3

Draw the map

From those answers we build a compact mathematical copy of the model, called a surrogate. The technique is a tensor train, borrowed from physics, which stays small even when an application has dozens of inputs.

Plain version: the map is a small, fast copy of the model that we can study without bothering the real one.
4

Mark where we're unsure

We test the map against fresh questions it has never seen and measure the worst error in every region. Where evidence is thin, where applications are unrealistic, or where the model jumps suddenly, we hatch the area and refuse to answer from the map.

This is the product's heart. Competitors give an answer. We give an answer plus a measured "how wrong could this be".
5

Explain any decision

Because the map has a special structure, we can calculate exactly how much each input pushed a decision, including combinations of inputs. On the model itself this would take an impossible number of questions (Chapter 3 shows why).

Who uses this: the lender, to write the reasons it must give Priya, and the bank's validators, to confirm the model looks at sensible things.
6

Hunt for bad behaviour

We search the whole map for places where the model does something it shouldn't: approval rising as debt rises, wild swings from tiny changes, unfair treatment of groups. A search method built for this map shape finds such spots far faster than trial and error.

Plain version: we look for potholes before a customer drives into one.
7

Hand over the report

Everything goes into a validation report written in the language of the regulator the customer answers to (RBI in India, the PRA in the UK, SR 26-2 in the US, the EU AI Act in Europe). Model vendors can also buy a certificate to show banks before a sale.

What we are not: we don't build loan models, we don't monitor chatbots, and we never replace the customer's model.
Chapter 3

Why the maths is the moat

To fairly split credit for a decision between inputs, the textbook method compares every possible combination of inputs. The number of combinations doubles with each input. Drag the slider.

20
Check every combination
Our map-based method

Bars use a logarithmic scale. Assumes one millisecond per model question. "Our method" is an illustrative estimate of questions needed to build the map for a model whose behaviour is fairly smoothassumption. The real number is what our benchmark must measure.

Real credit models often use 50 to 300 inputs. That is why today's tools sample a few combinations and hope, and why a study found their answers depend on the sampling.

Recent research shows explanations can be computed exactly and quickly once a model is written as a tensor network. One 2026 paper reports 25 to 1,000 times speed-ups over a standard method at similar accuracy, on public test data [A].

Honest limit: for tree-based models, a fast exact method already exists. Our edge is largest for neural networks, blended models and models we can only query.

Chapter 4

Why now: the rulebooks changed

In several major markets, regulators now expect lenders to check AI models, explain decisions, and take responsibility for vendor models. Some dates have slipped before, so we never build a plan around a single deadline.

Chapter 5

Who buys, and who else is out there

We start in India because the new RBI draft applies there, the team is there, and costs are low. Prices are higher in the UK and EU, so we go there second, through partners.

1

Banks that co-lend with NBFCs

The NBFC's model makes the first decision; the bank must check a model it can't open. That's our exact strength.

2

Companies that sell credit models

They pay for a certificate that makes bank due-diligence faster. A US company, ValidMind, already runs a programme like this.

3

Digital NBFCs with their own AI

Many of them, smaller budgets. An RBI survey found about one in five regulated entities using AI, mostly the large ones [C].

Chapter 7

The plan, with gates we must pass

Each gate has a pass test and a pre-agreed response if we fail. We decide the "what if it fails" now, so sunk costs don't decide for us later.

Gate 0, week 6

Is the pain real?

Pass: at least 6 of 25–30 interviews describe a named model that is either not tree-based or can only be queried, plus a named budget owner.
If not: lead with stress-testing and reports instead of explanations, or stop.
Gate 1, month 4

Does the maths win?

Pass: on neural and blended models, same accuracy as the standard tool with 10× fewer questions; flags 95% of weak areas; finds 90% of planted policy breaks.
If not: sell stress-testing only, or stop.
Gate 2, month 12

Will people pay?

Pass: 2 paid jobs done, and one customer confirms in writing they used our report.
If not: one more 6-month cycle with a different buyer type, then reassess.
Gate 3, month 24

Is it a business?

Pass: 12 paying customers plus one partner deal, or about US$0.4m yearly revenueassumption.
If not: keep the team flat, seek a partner or buyer.

    Chapter 10

    Words we use, in plain language

    Model
    A computer program that turns information about an applicant into a score or a decision.
    Black box, query-only
    A model we can send questions to and get answers from, but can't open.
    Surrogate
    Our compact copy of the model's behaviour, the "map". Fast to study, checked against the real thing.
    Tensor train
    The mathematical format of our map. It stays small even with many inputs, and makes exact explanations cheap.
    Certified error
    A measured statement of how far the map can be from the real model in each region.
    Shapley value
    A fair way to split credit for a decision between inputs, from game theory. The standard basis for "reason codes".
    Model validation
    An independent check that a model is sound, works as intended and is used correctly. Required by banking regulators.
    NBFC
    Non-banking financial company. An Indian lender that is not a bank, regulated by the RBI.
    Co-lending
    A bank and an NBFC funding the same loans together.
    SR 26-2, SS1/23
    The US and UK rulebooks for managing model risk at banks.
    EU AI Act
    Europe's AI law. Credit scoring counts as high-risk.
    Gate
    A planned checkpoint where we decide, using evidence, whether to continue, change, or stop.
